## Investment thesis

ASML sells the lithography tools chipmakers need to print advanced semiconductor features, then earns service and upgrade revenue from the installed base. Its unique EUV position and long qualification cycles give it strong economics when customers expand leading-edge capacity. The October Q3 release supports that demand case, but the price leaves little margin for a slower capacity or customer-spending cycle. The October 28 assessment is WATCH: the rebuilt weighted dollar value is only modestly above the completed-session quote. This review makes the currency translation and terminal dates explicit, removing a major limitation in the earlier model. The strongest opposing case is that tool scarcity, pricing and higher lithography intensity support a longer and more profitable growth period than the base forecast. The dependency is sustained customer investment and successful capacity delivery without disruptive export restrictions.

**Research date October 28, 2026.** Completed-session quote **$1,662.85**; independent comparison **$1,663.69**, difference 0.051%. After-hours prices are excluded from closing triggers. Model date October 28; terminal date 2028-12-31.

## How the business creates value

Customers pay for production systems that combine advanced optics, light sources and process control, plus service that keeps valuable fabrication capacity productive. EUV scarcity and a qualified supplier ecosystem are strong barriers to entry. High-NA adoption and more critical patterning exposures can raise content, while installed-base management creates a recurring revenue stream. Delivery capacity, supplier throughput, customer fab readiness and export approvals limit how quickly orders become revenue. The model's €64 billion FY2028 base revenue is about 44% above the €44.5 billion midpoint of 2026 guidance, requiring substantial additional output and service growth. That is an analyst forecast rather than issuer 2028 guidance.

### Five macro channels

- **Rates 1/5:** Higher discount rates reduce present value of equipment profits expected in later years.
- **Inflation 1/5:** Supplier and engineering costs matter, while pricing power may offset part of inflation.
- **Liquidity 4/5:** Customer funding access affects fabrication expansion and equipment orders.
- **Cycle 2/5:** Memory and logic capex cycles determine delivery schedules despite long-term demand.
- **Policy 1/5:** Export controls can restrict addressable tool and service sales directly.

The resulting macro assessment is **9/25**, an uptrend under pressure. V5.2 applies a 50% cap on normal exposure and starter/add pacing in this band; it does not mechanically reduce intrinsic value or create a stock-selection veto. The October 28 Fed decision kept the policy range at 3.75–4.00%; the official ten-year Treasury par yield was 5.05%. High rates remain material even after the rebound in equity indices.

## What could break the thesis

Tool scarcity can support orders while also delaying deliveries and revenue recognition. Customer concentration, export licensing and rapid fabrication-cycle changes can convert backlog into a less reliable earnings signal. High-NA must lower customers' manufacturing cost sufficiently to justify adoption; technical success alone does not establish attractive customer economics. Watch tool acceptance, service mix, capacity throughput, licensing restrictions and cash conversion. A 15% gap stress is an analyst planning case, not guaranteed protection.
