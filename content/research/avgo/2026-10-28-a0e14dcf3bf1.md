## How the business creates value

Broadcom designs custom AI accelerators and networking products for large customers and sells software that supports enterprise infrastructure. Technical integration, customer design relationships and software switching costs can sustain margins. Large production programs can also concentrate demand and expose working capital and capacity commitments to a few buyers.

A financing structure can accelerate customer deployment, allowing more chips and networking products to be delivered. Its shareholder benefit depends on the margin on those deliveries after financing support, credit losses, residual-value commitments and the cost of capital. Hardware that becomes obsolete rapidly may be worth less than lenders originally assumed. When the supplier supports that residual value, some customer-financing downside can return to the supplier even if ordinary chip manufacturing remains outsourced.

The October 26 announcement describes a $63 billion transaction using a special-purpose vehicle to purchase Broadcom products for lease to Anthropic, with deliveries expected to begin in calendar 2027. The accompanying [issuer presentation, pages 1–2](https://investors.broadcom.com/static-files/f4bda79d-cc66-4748-919b-40d08c658f2d) supplies material protection terms: potential liability is the difference between 72.5% of outstanding lease amounts and the value of AI racks received; expected peak exposure is $43 billion. The presentation describes no direct cash outlay and illustrates amortization to zero over five years of lease payments, with actual balances and timing dependent on deliveries and payments. The economic formula is therefore a conditional shortfall, max(72.5% × outstanding lease amount − received rack value, 0), rather than an immediate $43 billion or $63 billion cash deduction.

## What could break the thesis

The strongest protective case is that Anthropic meets its contractual payments while the equipment retains productive or releasable value. The issuer presentation identifies customer credit, contractual payments, re-leasing/redeployment and collateral value as protections ahead of support, and describes a low probability of a claim. Those are management's expectations rather than a measured default probability. If payments and asset values perform as intended, the transaction can enable profitable deliveries with limited support cost; a direct cash charge equal to gross exposure would overstate that case.

The strongest downside path is correlated customer and collateral stress. A customer that cannot profitably use the equipment may also be unable to pay as expected, while the equipment's resale value falls because technology advances or other capacity comes to market. Broadcom could then lose some semiconductor demand and incur support costs at the same time. That correlation matters more than treating the two risks as independent.

A second failure path is normal operating competition: customers can change chip designs, negotiate lower economics or delay deployments, and software customers can resist pricing or migrate workloads. Monitor customer concentration, realized cash collection, program delivery schedules, recurring software economics, credit support, collateral values and total debt. Strong technical trading cannot resolve missing contractual economics.
