## Investment thesis

Applied Optoelectronics sells lasers and optical transceivers that move data through cloud networks, alongside cable-network equipment. Faster AI networks could increase both the number and value of its optical links. Shareholders benefit only if added factories reach acceptable yields and produce cash after reinvestment; selling more modules while issuing more equity would weaken that per-share result. The latest released quarter still showed a GAAP loss, and the October financing illustrates that dependency. The October 28 assessment remains EXIT because the earlier $89 closing risk line failed and the rebound remains below it. The rebuilt scenario model also gives less value than the completed-session quote. The strongest opposing view is that 800G and 1.6T demand could absorb new capacity quickly enough to deliver the bull outcome. That remains a possible operating path rather than verified earnings.

**Research date October 28, 2026.** Completed-session quote **$82.22**; independent comparison **$82.23**, difference 0.012%. After-hours prices are excluded from closing triggers. Model date October 28; terminal date 2028-12-31.

## How the business creates value

Cloud operators buy transceivers to move traffic between computing equipment; cable customers buy amplifiers and related broadband products. AOI's internal laser and manufacturing capabilities can improve control over supply and product cost, but vertical integration also ties cash to equipment, work in progress and inventories. The transition to faster optical links creates opportunities for higher content per connection while allowing new suppliers or technologies to displace existing products. Customer qualification, yield and concentration are therefore more important than a broad AI spending headline. The base model requires $2.2 billion annual sales by FY2028, roughly twice the midpoint of the Q3 guidance annualized, with a 12% net margin. That bridge needs both volume growth and a move from loss to repeatable profitability; a single purchase announcement cannot establish it.

### Five macro channels

- **Rates 1/5:** Higher required returns reduce the value of profits expected after the capacity ramp.
- **Inflation 1/5:** Energy, labor and component costs can delay the move to positive net margins.
- **Liquidity 4/5:** Financing access matters because expansion precedes cash generation.
- **Cycle 2/5:** Cloud capex and cable upgrades determine utilization.
- **Policy 1/5:** Export rules and manufacturing geography can interrupt supply or customer access.

The resulting macro assessment is **9/25**, an uptrend under pressure. V5.2 applies a 50% cap on normal exposure and starter/add pacing in this band; it does not mechanically reduce intrinsic value or create a stock-selection veto. The October 28 Fed decision kept the policy range at 3.75–4.00%; the official ten-year Treasury par yield was 5.05%. High rates remain material even after the rebound in equity indices.

**Evidence gap:** A complete product/customer concentration schedule, competitor order comparison and synchronized industry-wide estimate-revision history were not established. These omissions limit confidence in the forecast bridge and industry score; the next filing and peer results should test them.

## What could break the thesis

The strongest failure mechanism is a mismatch between factory spending and customer acceptance. Lower yields or a concentrated customer's delay can leave inventories and fixed costs high just as selling prices fall. More equity could then finance growth without creating proportionate value per diluted unit. Watch the next filing for customer concentration, inventory growth, operating cash use and capital spending relative to sales. A 30% adverse gap is an analytical stress assumption, supported as a planning concern by the volatile price path; it is not a maximum loss or a guarantee of execution.
